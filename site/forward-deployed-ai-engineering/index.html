Forward-deployed engineering, accountable to an outcome.

What an FDE engagement is, when it fits, what you should receive at each gate, and how the commercial shape works.

What forward-deployed engineering is

A small team of DLI engineers works inside your team and workflow — not alongside it in a separate workstream — to design, integrate, validate and operate one AI system. The team is accountable to a baseline agreed at the start: a named process, a measured current cost or cycle time, and an acceptance test written before build begins.

That accountability is the difference. Staff augmentation gives you people and leaves the outcome with you. Advisory-only gives you a deck and leaves the operating system unbuilt. A product vendor gives you their product and asks you to adapt. Forward-deployed engineering gives you one team, one workflow, one baseline and a gate at every stage. When the work stops making sense, you can stop.

When it fits

Four conditions predict a workable engagement:

  • One workflow with a named owner who can make decisions and free up the people who run the process today.
  • Accessible systems — read access to the source records, and somewhere to deploy, even if that is a sandbox first.
  • A real baseline — a number, not an impression. Volume, cycle time, error rate, cost per case, or hours of rework.
  • A decision within a quarter. If the answer is needed in eighteen months, the scope is wrong.

When it does not. We decline, or reframe, when there is no owner (a committee is not an owner), when data access sits behind an approval no one can name, when the mandate is to build a platform before choosing the workflow it serves, or when the requirement is guaranteed determinism from a probabilistic system. The last one is not a negotiation. It is a different engineering problem, and in most cases it should be solved with rules, not a model.

The five stages and what you receive

Each stage ends at a decision gate. The artifacts below are illustrative of the form, not a fixed deliverable list; they are adapted to the workflow and your assurance requirements.

1. Discover

We map the process, the data, the owners and the constraints, and write the baseline down. Gate: is there a viable opportunity with an owner and a number? Artifact: opportunity brief.

2. Engineer

We design and build the integration, define least-privilege access for every tool the system can call, and document the choices that constrain later operation. Gate: does it do the work in a controlled test environment, with permissions agreed by your security owner? Artifacts: tool permission matrix, architecture decision record.

3. Validate

Evaluation sets are drawn from real cases, including the ugly ones — missing fields, ambiguous documents, edge conditions the demo never sees. Gate: does it meet the acceptance threshold, including on the failure cases? Artifact: evaluation scorecard.

4. Deploy

Release into the live workflow with human approval where the risk requires it, monitoring from the first transaction, and a rollback that has been rehearsed rather than promised. Gate: has the named owner signed the release? Artifact: release and rollback plan.

5. Operate

We run it: model routing, cost, incidents, drift, and a monthly review against the baseline. Gate: is the outcome still being met, and at what cost? Artifacts: operating runbook and monthly outcome report.

Commercial shape

Every engagement begins with a paid, time-boxed decision sprint — typically two weeks to reach one decision. It is deliberately small and produces the brief, the architecture options and the evaluation plan. You own the output whether or not you continue.

A first scoped workflow typically runs six to twelve weeks from that decision. Multi-entity, data-heavy or regulated mandates are phased after discovery, because the findings determine the sequence and the assurance work. There is no fixed delivery commitment before scope is confirmed in writing, and no public price list: the inputs — entities, systems, data classification, assurance requirements — vary too much for an honest number. You can stop at any gate.

Controls are part of the build

Identity and role-based access; data lineage from source to output; evaluation sets held as versioned assets; human approval at the points where an error is expensive or irreversible; logs sufficient to reconstruct a decision; a tested rollback path; model routing and cost visibility at the level of the workflow. These are engineering deliverables, not a compliance wrapper added at the end.

We do not claim a certification, and we do not claim guaranteed determinism. Where your obligations require an audit, a legal opinion or a sector approval, we prepare the evidence and work with your compliance and legal teams. The determination is theirs.

What we do not do

  • Staff augmentation without a named outcome and a baseline.
  • An “AI strategy” deck as the deliverable.
  • Vendor lock-in — the architecture is documented so another team can operate it.
  • Any claim of government partnership, client name, certification or performance result.

How to evaluate an FDE partner

Ask these five questions, of us or anyone else:

  1. What baseline will you be measured against, and who signs it?
  2. Who owns the system after the engagement ends, and what exactly do they receive?
  3. Can you show the evaluation set and the failure cases, not just the demo?
  4. What happens at each gate if the answer is no — and who decides?
  5. Which parts of the stack can we take in-house, and what does that cost us?

A partner who cannot answer the second and fourth in specific terms is selling staff, not an outcome.
